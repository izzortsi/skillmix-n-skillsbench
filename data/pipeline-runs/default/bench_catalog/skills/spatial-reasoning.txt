# spatial-reasoning

The capacity to reason about the spatial relationships between objects.

## When to Use

- Use when the challenge requires inferring a physical property, position, or arrangement of objects from relational cues in the passage rather than from direct statements.

## Procedure

1. Scan the passage for nouns referring to physical objects, containers, paths, or locations and list them.
2. For each pair of objects mentioned together, identify the spatial relation described (inside, above, beside, passing through, fitting into, blocking, etc.).
3. Translate each relation into an implied constraint on size, shape, position, or orientation (e.g., 'X fit into Y' implies X's relevant dimension ≤ Y's opening).
4. Check the challenge question against these derived constraints to see whether answering it requires inferring a spatial property not stated explicitly.
5. Combine multiple spatial constraints when needed to resolve the question, and verify the inference is consistent with every relation stated in the passage.

## Constraints

- Do not apply when the passage's relations are purely metaphorical or social rather than physical.
- Require that the inference follow from a stated spatial relation, not from world knowledge about the objects alone.
- Do not over-specify dimensions the passage leaves underdetermined; infer only the relation the text supports.
- One spatial word is not enough; confirm the relation actually constrains the property the question asks about.

## Example

Positive: A passage says a ladder reached the second-story window, and the question asks what can be inferred about the ladder's length. Why: the reaching relation constrains a physical dimension derivable from the stated arrangement.
Negative: A passage says 'the argument fit the occasion' and the question asks about the argument's suitability. Why: 'fit' is used metaphorically, so no physical spatial inference is warranted.
