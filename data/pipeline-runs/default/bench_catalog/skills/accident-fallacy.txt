# accident-fallacy

an informal fallacy and a deductively valid but unsound argument occurring in a statistical syllogism (an argument based on a generalization) when an exception to a rule of thumb is ignored.

## When to Use

- when asked to detect or diagnose an argument that applies a general rule to a specific case while overlooking a legitimate exception to that rule.

## Procedure

1. Locate the general rule or generalization stated in the passage (often a broad claim like 'X is always Y' or 'all X are Y').
2. Identify the specific case or instance the argument applies the rule to.
3. Check whether the specific case has features that plausibly qualify as a recognized exception to the general rule (context, role, intent, legal permission, etc.).
4. Determine whether the argument ignores or suppresses that exception when drawing its conclusion about the specific case.
5. Confirm the argument's form is a statistical or rule-of-thumb syllogism rather than a universally true premise, and flag it only if an overlooked exception is what drives the faulty conclusion.

## Constraints

- Do not flag arguments whose major premise is genuinely exceptionless rather than a generalization.
- Do not flag arguments where the specific case does not actually fall under a recognized exception.
- Require that both a generalization and an ignored qualifying exception be identifiable; missing either element disqualifies the match.
- Do not confuse this with hasty generalization, which moves from cases to a rule rather than from a rule to a case.

## Example

Positive: An argument claims 'Taking others' property is theft; tax collectors take others' property; therefore tax collectors are thieves.' Why: It applies a rule-of-thumb to a case while ignoring the legal-authorization exception.
Negative: An argument claims 'All triangles have three sides; this figure is a triangle; therefore it has three sides.' Why: The major premise is universally true with no exceptions, so no exception is being overlooked.
