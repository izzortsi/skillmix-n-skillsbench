# self-serving-bias

A cognitive or perceptual process that is distorted by the need to maintain and enhance one's self esteem.

## When to Use

- When asked to detect asymmetric causal attribution in which a speaker credits themselves for positive outcomes while blaming external factors for negative ones.

## Procedure

1. Locate each statement in the passage where a person or group explains the cause of an outcome involving themselves.
2. Classify each outcome as favorable (success, praise, gain) or unfavorable (failure, blame, loss) for the speaker.
3. For each attribution, note whether the cause cited is internal (ability, effort, character) or external (luck, others, environment, conditions).
4. Flag the pattern only when favorable outcomes are paired with internal causes AND unfavorable outcomes are paired with external causes by the same subject.
5. Verify the pattern serves self-esteem protection or enhancement, rather than being justified by evidence presented in the passage.
6. Confirm at least one success-internal and one failure-external pairing coexist before marking the pattern as present.

## Constraints

- Do not flag cases where only successes or only failures are discussed; both polarities must appear.
- Do not flag attributions that the passage independently supports with evidence, even if they favor the speaker.
- Do not flag third-party attributions; the speaker and the subject of the outcome must be the same.
- A single self-congratulatory or blame-shifting statement alone is insufficient without the contrasting pair.

## Example

Positive: A manager says her team's successful quarter reflects her leadership, but the failed quarter was due to market conditions beyond her control. Why: Success is attributed internally and failure externally by the same subject, protecting self-image.
Negative: A student says he passed because he studied hard and failed another exam because he didn't study enough. Why: Both outcomes receive internal attributions, so there is no self-protective asymmetry.
