# modus-ponens

A syllogism that is of the form 'If P then Q. P. Hence Q.'

## When to Use

- when asked to evaluate whether a conclusion validly follows from a conditional premise paired with an affirmed antecedent.

## Procedure

1. Scan the passage for a conditional statement of the form 'if X then Y' (or equivalent phrasings like 'whenever X, Y' or 'X implies Y').
2. Identify the antecedent (the 'if' part) and the consequent (the 'then' part) of that conditional.
3. Search the passage for a separate assertion that the antecedent is true or has occurred.
4. Confirm the asserted fact matches the antecedent exactly, not a related or weaker claim.
5. If both the conditional and its affirmed antecedent are present, derive or select the consequent as the valid conclusion.
6. Reject the inference if the passage affirms the consequent, denies the antecedent, or introduces a different conditional structure.

## Constraints

- Do not apply this pattern if only the consequent is asserted as true; that is a different and invalid inference.
- Require both a conditional statement and an explicit affirmation of its antecedent before concluding.
- Do not treat probabilistic or causal-sounding sentences as conditionals unless they establish a strict 'if-then' relationship.
- Do not substitute a paraphrased or broader claim for the antecedent; the match must be exact in meaning.

## Example

Positive: A passage states 'If it rains, the match is canceled' and separately asserts 'It is raining,' prompting the student to infer the match is canceled. Why: the conditional and its affirmed antecedent are both present, yielding a valid consequent.
Negative: A passage states 'If it rains, the match is canceled' and separately asserts 'The match was canceled,' prompting an inference that it rained. Why: only the consequent is affirmed, which does not license concluding the antecedent.
