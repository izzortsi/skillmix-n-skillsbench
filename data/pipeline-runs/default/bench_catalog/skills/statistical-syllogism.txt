# statistical-syllogism

A syllogism that argues, using inductive reasoning, from a generalization true for the most part to a particular case.

## When to Use

- Use when asked to detect, classify, or evaluate an argument that moves from a 'most members have property P' premise plus an individual's group membership to a conclusion about that individual.

## Procedure

1. Scan the passage for a generalization that holds for most (but not all) members of a group, using quantifiers like 'almost all', 'the majority', 'usually', or 'most'.
2. Locate a statement asserting that a specific individual or instance is a member of that group.
3. Check whether a conclusion is drawn (or is being asked) that applies the general trait to that specific member.
4. Verify the inference is inductive (probabilistic) rather than deductive: the premise must allow exceptions, not claim universality.
5. Confirm the three pieces align: the group in the generalization, the group in the membership claim, and the trait attributed to the individual must all match.
6. Flag or apply the pattern only when all three components (majority premise, membership premise, individual conclusion) are present and connected.

## Constraints

- Do not flag arguments whose major premise is universal ('all' or 'none') rather than statistical.
- Do not flag a standalone generalization that lacks an individual-case conclusion.
- Require the group referenced in the generalization to be the same group the individual is said to belong to.
- Do not treat a conclusion stated with certainty as equivalent when the premise is only probabilistic; note the inductive gap.

## Example

Positive: 'Most commuters in this city own a transit pass. Lena is a commuter here, so she likely has a transit pass.' Why: a majority premise plus individual membership yields a probabilistic conclusion about that individual.
Negative: 'All mammals have lungs. A whale is a mammal, so a whale has lungs.' Why: the major premise is universal, making this a deductive categorical syllogism rather than an inductive inference from a statistical majority.
