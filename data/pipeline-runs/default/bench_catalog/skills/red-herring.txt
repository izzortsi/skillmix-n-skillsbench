# red-herring

Introducing irrelevant points to detract attention from a question.

## When to Use

- when asked to detect a diversion in which a responder substitutes an irrelevant but appealing topic for a direct answer to a posed question.

## Procedure

1. Locate the explicit question or challenge posed in the passage and write down its core subject.
2. For each response sentence, identify its actual topic and compare it to the question's subject.
3. Mark any response segment that shifts to a broadly appealing or tangential topic without answering the core subject.
4. Check whether the shift uses positive or emotionally resonant language to make the diversion seem like a natural reply.
5. Confirm that the original question remains unaddressed after the diversion before concluding the pattern applies.

## Constraints

- Do not flag a response that genuinely addresses the question even if it includes additional praise or context.
- Require both a clear question and a clear topic shift; a vague or partial answer alone is not sufficient.
- Do not flag off-topic remarks that occur without any prior question prompting them.
- Do not rely on tone alone; the response must fail to engage the question's actual subject.

## Example

Positive: A reporter asks a CEO why quarterly profits dropped, and the CEO replies that the company is deeply committed to innovation and employee wellbeing. Why: the reply swaps the specific profit question for unrelated positive themes without addressing it.
Negative: A reporter asks a CEO why quarterly profits dropped, and the CEO explains supply-chain delays before adding that the company remains committed to innovation. Why: the core question is directly answered, with the positive remark as supplement rather than substitution.
